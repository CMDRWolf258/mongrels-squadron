New Commander

Ask four questions first.

Which system? Which faction? What exact result do we want? What action actually moves that result? Those four answers prevent most accidental BGS damage.

Operator Standard

Every order needs a stop condition.

“Support the faction” is not an operational plan. Good orders specify the beneficiary, the lever, an approximate workload, what to avoid, and when to stop.

Starter Guide

The BGS in Six Steps

This is the basic operating loop. Everything else in the manual is a deeper version of one of these steps.

01

Read the System

Check every faction, not just your own.

  • Influence and controller.
  • Asset ownership.
  • Active + pending states.
  • Conflicts and faction count.
02

Define the Result

“Help our faction” is too vague.

  • Raise or lower influence?
  • Trigger or avoid conflict?
  • Push economy/security?
  • Take or protect an asset?
03

Choose the Lever

Pick the activity that moves the intended dimension.

  • Missions / INF.
  • Trade / exploration.
  • Bounties / security.
  • Conflict-specific actions.
04

Set the Limit

Specify a workload and a stop condition before you start. Near thresholds, small corrections are safer than heroic overwork.

05

Let the Tick Resolve

Record what was done, then compare the post-tick result. Data sites may lag until a CMDR visits and uploads fresh journals.

06

Recalibrate

Use the result to tune tomorrow’s orders. BGS is a control problem, not a fixed “X missions = Y%” recipe.

Influence & Levers

Move the Right Dimension

Influence, economy, security, and conflict score are related but distinct. One activity can touch several at once.

Mission INF is not system influence %. A mission showing INF +++++ gives a relative influence reward weight. It does not mean +5 percentage points.
ActivityInfluence / state useConflict useOperational caution
MissionsTargeted support; mission type can also move economy/security.Combat missions can help War/Civil War; non-combat/economic missions can help Elections.Read issuer, target, destination, and reward. One mission can help one faction while hurting another.
BountiesPositive faction + security pressure for the issuing faction.Can contribute to War/Civil War scoring.Mixed vouchers and jurisdiction can muddy precise targeting.
Profitable tradePositive economic/influence pressure for the market owner.Can contribute to Election scoring.The asset owner matters more than “being in the right system.”
Exploration dataPositive support through the receiving faction-owned asset.Can contribute to Election scoring.Check ownership before spending valuable data.
Smuggling / black marketOften used for negative economic pressure against the market owner.Economic/smuggling activity can contribute during Elections.Government ethos and station services determine availability; effects can be strategically messy.
Violent crimeNegative influence/security pressure against the victim.Can contribute to War/Civil War scoring.Notoriety, legal consequences, and unwanted security states may outweigh the benefit.
Veteran notes — population, saturation, and opposition

Population changes how resistant a system feels, and repeated activity is subject to practical diminishing returns. Opposing CMDRs and random traffic are added on top. Treat published workloads as starting points, not formulas. For an important system, log a known batch of work, observe the tick, and build your own local calibration.

Conflicts

Best of Seven — Not “Grind Until the Bar Moves”

War, Civil War, and Election use separate daily conflict scoring. Ordinary displayed influence is not the scoreboard you are trying to win.

FormatBest of 7 daily results
Maximum7 days
Early endingWhen the loser cannot force a draw
Score updateAt the BGS tick

How the early-ending math works

After the tickDays remainingCan the trailer force a draw?Result
4 wins – 0 wins3No. Best possible comeback is 4–3.Ends after Day 4.
2 wins – 0 wins, 4 ties1No. Best possible comeback is 2–1.Can end after Day 6.
2 wins – 2 wins, 2 ties1Yes.Day 7 still matters.
3 wins – 3 wins1Yes.Day 7 decides unless it ties.
A huge victory does not buy extra days. Frontier removed bonus-day wins. The value of overwhelming a day is insurance against hidden opposition, not multiple points on the series score.

Before the Conflict

Identify both governments/ethos groups, the asset at risk, whether system control is at stake, and whether you actually want this opponent.

During the Conflict

Track the completed daily score after each tick. You cannot see the opponent’s live work, so current-day “safety” is always an estimate.

After the Conflict

Verify the asset transfer, new influence separation, recovery/cooldown, and whether another crossover is now possible.

Veteran note — Theocracy and legacy factions

Some Frontier-created or older factions can have hidden/multiple ethos behavior that does not line up neatly with the visible government label. Theocracy is the classic caution. If a campaign depends on the exact conflict type, check the faction’s observed history instead of assuming the table is infallible.

Control & Assets

Know What You Are Actually Fighting Over

System control, station ownership, and settlement ownership are related, but they are not the same thing.

ConceptMeaningWhy it matters
System controllerThe faction holding the controlling asset / top political position for the system.Determines the system’s controlling faction and many system-level conditions.
Asset ownerThe faction that owns a particular station, outpost, installation, or settlement.Trade, data, missions, and services at that asset feed that owner—not automatically the system controller.
Asset at riskThe asset staked for transfer in a conflict.A normal conflict does not automatically transfer every asset owned by the losing faction.
Control conflictA conflict where the controlling asset/control position is at stake.Winning can change the system controller.
Pre-conflict checklist: identify the opponent, conflict type, asset at risk, whether control is at stake, and what the post-conflict influence order will probably look like.

Expansion & Retreat

Thresholds Need Safety Margins

The top and bottom of the influence range can change where a faction exists. Treat the observed thresholds as strategic boundaries, not targets to sit directly on.

MechanicObserved threshold / rulePractical applicationCommon mistake
ExpansionRoughly 75% influence in an eligible origin system.If wanted, map the neighborhood first. If unwanted, hold below the trigger with margin.Crossing 75% and only afterward asking where the faction might expand.
Pending ExpansionCurrent community guidance treats it as committed once pending.Shift from “prevent” to “prepare/manage destination.”Assuming one negative tick can cancel it after pending.
RetreatBelow roughly 2.5% for a non-native faction.Save with a decisive buffer; remove by preventing accidental support.Aiming exactly for 2.5% or waiting until the last apparent day.
Native factionCannot normally Retreat from its home system.Use another strategic method if the faction is native.Trying to remove a native faction with Retreat.
Faction capSeven factions in a normal system.Full systems change expansion targeting and invasion possibilities.Planning an expansion as though every nearby system has an open slot.
Veteran notes — expansion targeting

Expansion targeting is a neighborhood problem, not a destination selector. Distance, open slots, faction counts, existing/previous faction presence, active states, and invasion eligibility can all matter. Because Frontier has not published a complete targeting formula, the Reference Database marks the detailed targeting model as community-observed.

Faction States

Read Cause, Effect, and Response Together

A state label is useful only if you know what it means operationally. Multiple compatible states can be active at the same time.

Practical Operations

From Situation Report to Daily Order

This is the workflow that turns the theory above into something a CMDR can actually execute.

1Snapshot

Record current influence, control, assets, active/pending states, conflict score, and faction count.

2Objective

Write the desired post-tick condition: e.g. “Faction A up, Faction B down, security up, remain below Expansion.”

3Levers

Pick actions that serve the objective and avoid actions that accidentally push the wrong slider or conflict.

4Quantity

Give a measured target: mission INF, bounty value, trade tonnage/profit, CZ wins, or specific election missions.

5Stop condition

State when to stop: workload reached, conflict day secured enough for the risk level, or threshold buffer reached.

6Post-tick review

Compare result to input and adjust tomorrow. Preserve the log so the system becomes easier to predict.

Example daily orders

Influence + Security“Turn in ~20M Mongrel bounties; complete ~20–30 mission INF for Mongrels; avoid crime against Mongrels; stop after target workload.”
War Day“Win CZs for Faction A; hand in bonds/combat missions before tick; do not spend the session on trade/exploration expecting it to win the war day.”
Election Day“Prioritize non-combat election missions for Faction A; add trade/exploration/economic support; avoid combat as the primary lever.”
Avoid Expansion“Support selected minority faction; no Mongrel positive work after the planned amount; maintain a safety buffer below ~75%.”
Mongrel doctrine: check Daily Orders before doing BGS in a priority system. Leadership may intentionally be lowering, holding, or withholding support from the squad faction.

Living manual

BGS is partly documented by Frontier and partly reverse-engineered by long-running community testing. This manual treats Frontier-published conflict behavior as firm, labels community-observed thresholds and targeting rules, and avoids presenting uncertain trigger formulas as fact. We expect to refine this guide as the squad encounters edge cases and as Frontier changes the simulation.
